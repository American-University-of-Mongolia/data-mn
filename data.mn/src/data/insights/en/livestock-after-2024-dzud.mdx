Mongolia counted **58.1 million head of livestock at the end of 2025** — almost exactly the same as a year earlier, and 13 million head below the 2022 peak of 71.1 million. After two years of heavy losses, the national herd has stopped falling. It has not begun to recover.

## Two bad years, back to back

The herd fell **9.1% in 2023** and a further **10.9% in 2024** — a combined loss of about 13.5 million animals in two winters. The 2024 fall alone is the third-steepest single-year drop in the 55 years of records.

Only two episodes were worse. In 2010, the herd fell **25.6%** in a single year, from 44.0 to 32.7 million head. And across 1999 to 2002, a run of consecutive bad winters cost **28.8%** of the national herd.

Those earlier episodes offer a sobering guide to how long recovery takes. After the 2010 collapse, it took until **2013** for the herd to return to its 2009 level — three years to make up one winter.

## Sheep and goats took the losses

The damage was not spread evenly across species. Comparing 2025 with the 2022 peak:

| Livestock | 2022 | 2025 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sheep | 32.7M | 23.9M | **-27.1%** |
| Goats | 27.6M | 23.2M | **-16.0%** |
| Cattle | 5.5M | 5.4M | -1.3% |
| Horses | 4.8M | 5.1M | +6.4% |
| Camels | 0.47M | 0.50M | +6.5% |

Sheep and goats account for nearly all of the loss. Horse and camel numbers actually grew across the same period. Small stock are more vulnerable to deep cold and blocked grazing, and they are also the animals herders sell or slaughter first when fodder runs short.

The goat share matters for household income, since cashmere is a major export earner. A 16% smaller goat herd is a direct hit to herder cash flow that lasts well beyond the winter that caused it.

## 2025: the west still falling, the Gobi growing

The flat national figure hides two opposite regional stories. Among aimags, the largest declines in 2025 were:

- **Darkhan-Uul: -35.3%**
- **Selenge: -22.1%**
- **Uvs: -11.0%**
- **Bayan-Ulgii: -9.7%**
- **Khovd: -8.8%**

Meanwhile several Gobi and eastern aimags grew: **Umnugovi +16.5%**, **Sukhbaatar +15.0%**, **Dornogovi +13.2%** and **Govisumber +11.6%**.

The western aimags — Uvs, Bayan-Ulgii, Khovd — form a contiguous block that was still losing animals in 2025, while the south and east were rebuilding. Darkhan-Uul and Selenge are small-herd farming provinces where percentage swings are large in absolute terms but modest in animal numbers.

## Fodder costs stayed high

Hay prices give a partial read on how hard the winter was to get through. In December 2025, a bale of hay averaged about **14,600 MNT** across the 14 aimags reporting prices, roughly **10% above** the previous published reading.

Prices vary enormously by aimag, and coverage is uneven — only a handful of aimags report loose hay and green feed prices in any given month. That patchiness is a real limitation: the series is best read as a regional spot check, not a national fodder price index.

## The winter itself was mild

One thing the 2025-26 winter was not is unusually cold. Measured against the 1981-2010 baseline, monthly average temperatures ran **warmer** than normal through the cold season: +0.5°C in December 2025, **+2.6°C in January 2026** and +1.1°C in February.

This is worth stating plainly because it cuts against intuition. A mild winter is not the same as a safe winter, and average temperature is one of the weaker predictors of a bad year for herders.

## What these numbers cannot tell you

Dzud is not a temperature reading. It is a combination of conditions: a dry summer that leaves animals entering winter underweight, deep or crusted snow that seals off grazing, ice layers over pasture, and herd sizes already beyond what the pasture can carry.

The data on this site covers **none of those directly**. We hold annual livestock counts, monthly station temperatures and patchy regional fodder prices. We do not hold pasture biomass, snow depth, ice cover or summer rainfall.

So these indicators describe **what happened to the herd**, not how much risk the coming winter holds. Official dzud risk assessments for Mongolia are produced by the National Agency for Meteorology and Environmental Monitoring (NAMEM), which combines the pasture and snow measurements that this kind of forecast requires. Nothing on this page should be read as a risk forecast or a substitute for one.
